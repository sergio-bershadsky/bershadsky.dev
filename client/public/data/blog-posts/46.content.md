# Comprehension Debt: Boundaries Beat Willpower

> You can't out-read a machine writing a thousand lines an hour. Plugin contracts are the only comprehension strategy that survives code you didn't write.

Your team cannot out-read a machine that writes a thousand lines an hour. Stop trying. The only comprehension strategy that survives code you didn't write is a boundary the code cannot cross — and we've known how to draw one since 1972.

That's the loud version. The quiet version: willpower-based review lost an arms race it was never built to win, and the teams still coping didn't learn to read faster — they changed the unit of comprehension from the line to the contract. This part makes that argument properly: first the cognitive-science canon that says your head was never big enough, then the plugin ecosystems that prove contracts substitute for reading at industrial scale, then the tooling that turns a boundary from a diagram into something that can fail your build. By the end you should be able to defend the claim with sources — and wire up the enforcement before lunch.

## The Arms Race You Already Lost

[Part 1](/blog/comprehension-debt-the-debt-nobody-booked) put numbers on the imbalance, so one paragraph of recap will do. Program comprehension consumed [about 58% of professional developer time before AI wrote a single line](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7997917/). Then generation went industrial: [GitClear measured duplicated code blocks up 8x in 2024](https://www.gitclear.com/ai_assistant_code_quality_2025_research) while refactoring collapsed, [METR's trial found experienced developers 19% slower with AI while believing they were 20% faster](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/), and [AI-assisted engineers scored 17% lower on comprehension quizzes about code they had just shipped](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245). Reading was already the majority cost of software; the writing side then multiplied.

Almost every team meets this with the same first move: review harder. Longer checklists, a second approver, a solemn agreement to *actually read* the generated diffs this time. Notice what these have in common — they are promises. A promise is not a mechanism. It has no throughput, it degrades under deadline pressure, and it quietly renegotiates itself at six on a Friday. The other side of the arms race doesn't get tired, doesn't get reassigned, and doesn't have Fridays. You don't beat a machine with virtue. You beat it with structure.

> The review queue is where good intentions go to age.

## The Two-to-Six-Chunk Head

Here's what your intuition already suspects and the literature confirms: the reading instrument is small, and it does not scale. Felienne Hermans' [The Programmer's Brain](https://www.manning.com/books/the-programmers-brain) summarizes the cognitive-science consensus — working memory holds roughly **two to six chunks** at a time. Not two to six files. Chunks. The expert advantage isn't a bigger buffer; it's better compression: a senior engineer reads an idiomatic for-loop as one chunk where a novice reads eleven tokens. Comprehension at scale was therefore never about reading everything. It's about compressing the system into units that fit a six-slot head.

Which is precisely what the architecture canon has been building, one generation at a time. [Parnas, 1972](https://wstomv.win.tue.nl/edu/2ip30/references/criteria_for_modularization.pdf): decompose systems around the decisions likely to change and hide them, so a module can be written — his words — with little knowledge of the code in another module. [Evans' bounded contexts](https://martinfowler.com/bliki/BoundedContext.html): a model only has to be coherent inside its boundary, so nobody holds the whole domain at once. [Team Topologies](https://teamtopologies.com/key-concepts): size ownership to the cognitive load a team can actually carry — load as an architecture sizing unit, not an HR metric. Fifty years, one theme: every serious school of software design is a **working-memory adapter**. The boundary isn't where the system gets divided; it's where comprehension gets compressed.

None of these people were designing for LLMs, and that's exactly why the canon holds up now. It was derived from the constraints of the reader — and the reader's constraints didn't move when the writer became a machine.

> Working memory ships with six slots and no upgrade path. The architecture has to route around it.

## The Plugin Proof

If contracts genuinely substitute for reading, there should exist ecosystems where thousands of engineers extend enormous codebases they have never read. There are several, and you used one today. Maël Nison rebuilt Yarn around a plugin core and reported that freshly onboarded contributors ship features ["without having to be aware of all the subtle details of the core implementation"](https://dev.to/arcanis/plugin-systems-when-why-58pp). [VS Code](https://code.visualstudio.com/api) runs tens of thousands of extensions against a deliberately narrow API — authors ship daily against millions of lines of editor they have never opened. An [ESLint rule](https://eslint.org/docs/latest/extend/custom-rules) is a small module visiting an AST its author didn't parse; a [Vite plugin](https://vite.dev/guide/api-plugin) is a handful of named hooks into a build pipeline its author will never step through. In every case the comprehension surface is the contract, not the codebase.

> Nobody has read all of VS Code. The extensions work anyway.

On the left, trusting a change means holding every module and every edge — a surface that grows with the codebase and blew past a human head years ago. On the right, the internals go grey: the reviewer reads one violet contract, and the contract's guarantees do the rest. Same system, different comprehension surface — one of them fits the instrument that has to do the comprehending.

Worth being precise about what makes the trick work, because "we have interfaces" is not it. The contract substitutes for reading only when it carries three things: the types (what crosses the boundary), the hooks (when you get called), and the invariants (what the core guarantees no matter what you do). Miss the third and the boundary is decorative — every consumer ends up reading the implementation anyway, just to learn what it silently promises. The plugin ecosystems above earn their comprehension discount by paying for it in specification.

And the same medicine works on the machine reader. [dasroot's "Code for the AI Reader"](https://dasroot.net/posts/2026/05/code-for-ai-reader-redesigning-architecture-llm-era/) argues that LLM-era architecture should trade clever idioms for explicit structure — narrow, single-responsibility modules a model can reason about without loading the world. An agent handed a contract and a bounded slice performs better than one handed the monorepo, for the same reason you do: context is a budget. InfoQ now treats [comprehension itself as an architectural characteristic](https://www.infoq.com/articles/system-comprehension-evolutionary-architecture/) — "a system that is not understood cannot evolve safely" — and its named failure mode is boundary erosion. The day one import reaches around the port, local reasoning dies, and reading everything becomes mandatory again.

## Boundaries That Go Red

A boundary that lives in a diagram is [Part 2's problem](/blog/comprehension-debt-frozen-context) all over again: prose that cannot fail, drifting toward fiction. The fix is the one that part promised — give the boundary a failing state. The evolutionary-architecture school calls these [fitness functions](https://www.thoughtworks.com/insights/articles/fitness-function-driven-development): executable checks on architectural properties, run in CI like any other test. Boundary checks happen to be the cheapest fitness functions on the market: [dependency-cruiser](https://github.com/sverweij/dependency-cruiser) validates every import in a JS/TS codebase against declared rules, [eslint-plugin-boundaries](https://github.com/javierbrea/eslint-plugin-boundaries) does it inside the linter you already run, and [ArchUnit](https://www.archunit.org/) does the same for the JVM as plain unit tests. Declaring the boundary takes about a dozen lines:

```javascript
// eslint.config.js — the boundary, declared once
{
  settings: {
    "boundaries/elements": [
      { type: "ui", pattern: "src/ui/**" },
      { type: "core-api", pattern: "src/core/api/**" },
      { type: "core-internals", pattern: "src/core/internals/**" },
    ],
  },
  rules: {
    "boundaries/element-types": ["error", {
      default: "disallow",
      rules: [{ from: "ui", allow: ["core-api"] }],
      // ui -> core-internals is now a lint error, not a review hope
    }],
  },
}
```

That config is everything the markdown KB wasn't. When an agent — or a hurried human — writes `import { resolve } from "../core/internals/resolver"`, nothing depends on a reviewer noticing. The linter notices. The build exits 1. The diff does not merge, however confident its author and however plausible the code. The boundary holds at machine speed against a machine writer, which is the entire requirement.

Top half is the deal, bottom half is the muscle. The emerald arrow through the public API is business as usual; the rose import into the internals never survives to review, because the declared rules feed a checker that feeds a gate. Nothing in this stack depends on anyone's attention span — which is the property [Part 2](/blog/comprehension-debt-frozen-context) showed markdown could never have. A boundary written in prose informs; a boundary written in config binds.

Where to start matters less than that the gate exists, but if you want the highest-yield first move: draw one boundary around the module nobody currently understands — the generated one, usually — and give it a public directory and a forbidden one. One rule, one red build the first week, and the team learns viscerally that the wall is load-bearing.

Honesty clause, because plugins are not free and nobody should sell them that way. Contract design is genuinely hard — Parnas-hiding the *right* decisions takes taste, and a port drawn in the wrong place is a wall through your own living room. A published contract is also a promise you must version like one: Yarn and VS Code spend real engineering budget keeping their plugin APIs stable, because breaking a contract breaks every consumer that trusted it. Boundaries are dramatically cheaper than reading everything. They are not cheap.

> Reviewers have Fridays. Exit codes don't.

## What "Understood" Means Now

Here's what actually changes for a team that takes this seriously: the definition of *understood*. The old definition — someone has read every line — died quietly somewhere in [Part 1's data](/blog/comprehension-debt-the-debt-nobody-booked), and pretending otherwise is how comprehension debt compounds. The workable definition is structural: every module sits behind a contract, every contract has an owner who can explain it from memory, and every boundary is enforced by something that goes red when crossed. Understanding stops being a heroic property of individual readers and becomes a property of the system — inspectable, assignable, checkable in CI.

Notice what this does to Naur's problem. [The theory that can't be written down](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/Naur.pdf) doesn't have to cover the whole system anymore — it has to cover the contracts and the seams, which is a load a team can actually hold and hand over. That's the possibility worth building toward: an engineer who trusts the boundaries can hold a multi-million-line system in a six-chunk head, not as a compromise but as the design — the exact arrangement under which every VS Code extension author already works, cheerfully, every day. AI can write most of your lines while humans hold all of your contracts, and both sides of that deal get the reader they need. One gap remains, and it's the interesting one: a boundary says where code may reach, but nothing about what the system should *do*. Giving that intent a failing state too is Part 4's job.
