# Comprehension Debt: The Debt Nobody Booked

> AI writes code faster than your team builds the theory of it. The gap is a debt, it compounds, and it isn't on any ledger your tools can see.

Your codebase grew eight times faster than anyone read it, and the ledger says everything is fine. It isn't. AI didn't write you bad code — it wrote you code whose theory no human has ever held. That's not technical debt. That's a debt nobody even booked.

Big claim, so let's put numbers on it before it starts sounding like another anti-AI sermon. This series is not one — I ship AI-generated code daily and intend to keep doing it. This first part does three things: shows you the 2025–2026 data, hands you the forty-year-old paper that predicted all of it, and names the debt precisely enough that the rest of the series can pay it down.

## The Numbers Nobody Wants

Start with the baseline, because it predates the hype entirely. In 2018 — three years before Copilot — [Xia et al. measured 78 professional developers across seven industrial projects](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7997917/) and found that **about 58% of developer time goes to program comprehension**. Not writing. Reading, tracing, rebuilding an understanding of what's already there. Comprehension was the majority cost of software before a single line was ever generated.

Now the generated lines. [GitClear's analysis of 211 million changed lines](https://www.gitclear.com/ai_assistant_code_quality_2025_research) from 2020 to 2024 found duplicated code blocks up **8x** in 2024 alone, copy-pasted lines climbing from 8.3% to 12.3%, and refactored ("moved") lines collapsing from 24.1% to 9.5%. 2024 was the first year in their dataset where copy/paste exceeded refactoring — the codebase equivalent of a country importing more than it exports and calling it growth. Their [2026 follow-up](https://www.gitclear.com/the_ai_code_quality_maintainability_gap) names the trend directly: a maintainability gap.

The productivity side is stranger. [METR's randomized controlled trial](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/) put experienced open-source developers on real tasks in repos they knew deeply — and with AI assistance they were **19% slower, while believing they were 20% faster**. Honest caveat: [METR's own 2026 update](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/) with a larger cohort moderates that to roughly break-even. But the perception gap survived both studies, and the perception gap is the point: we are systematically miscalibrated about what AI assistance costs us.

The rest of the industry data rhymes. [DORA 2025](https://dora.dev/) finds AI adoption near-universal (~90%) and positively correlated with throughput — but still negatively correlated with delivery stability. [Stack Overflow's 2025 survey](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/) has 84% of developers using or planning to use AI while 46% distrust its output, with "almost-right answers" the top frustration. And [Anthropic's own skill-formation study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20245) found AI-assisted engineers scoring 17% lower on comprehension quizzes about the very code they'd just shipped.

> The review approved the diff. Nobody reviewed the theory.

## A Program Is a Theory

Here's the part that was predicted in 1985. Peter Naur — the N in BNF — wrote a short paper called [Programming as Theory Building](https://pages.cs.wisc.edu/~remzi/Naur.pdf) arguing that the primary product of programming is not the source code. It is the **theory**: the mental model, held by the people who built the system, of how the program maps onto the world it serves. Why this boundary sits here. Which similarity between two functions is meaningful and which is coincidence. What must never happen, and what merely doesn't happen yet.

Naur's sharpest claim is that this theory *cannot be fully written down*. Documentation captures conclusions; the theory is the reasoning that produced them, and it degrades on serialization. His grimmest claim follows directly: a program effectively **dies** when the team holding its theory disperses. The source code survives, compiles, even runs — but nobody can safely change it, because change requires the theory, and the theory left in the elevator.

> Git stores everything except the reason.

Forty years later this paper is having a revival, and not among nostalgists. [Sean Goedecke reads agent-assisted programming through Naur](https://www.seangoedecke.com/programming-with-ai-agents-as-theory-building/) and concludes the human's job is now *only* the theory. Geoffrey Litt argues [understanding is the new bottleneck](https://sparsenotes.com/posts/2026/07/understanding-is-the-new-bottleneck/) — generation got cheap, so verification and comprehension became the constraint. The reason Naur suddenly matters again is uncomfortable and simple: an LLM produces code for which *no theory was ever held by anyone*. Not lost. Never formed.

## Born-Legacy Code

Michael Feathers famously defined legacy code as [code without tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_Effectively_with_Legacy_Code). The AI-era extension writes itself: legacy code is code without *theory* — and by that definition, unreviewed generated code is **born legacy**. It skips the decades of drift and arrives pre-orphaned, day one, with green CI.

The term for the accumulating consequence is recent. [Jason Gorman coined "comprehension debt" in September 2025](https://codemanship.wordpress.com/2025/09/30/comprehension-debt-the-ticking-time-bomb-of-llm-generated-code/) — the future time-cost of having to understand code before you can safely change it. [Addy Osmani's March 2026 essay](https://addyosmani.com/blog/comprehension-debt/) (republished by [O'Reilly Radar](https://www.oreilly.com/radar/comprehension-debt-the-hidden-cost-of-ai-generated-code/)) made it mainstream, defining it as "the growing gap between how much code exists in a system and how much of it any human genuinely understands," and by April 2026 it had [its own arXiv paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.13277).

The aftermath file is short but instructive: [Replit's agent deleted a production database during an explicit code freeze](https://www.theregister.com/2025/07/21/replit_saastr_vibe_coding_incident/); [Escape.tech scanned 5,600 vibe-coded apps and found over 2,000 high-impact vulnerabilities and 400+ exposed secrets](https://escape.tech/state-of-security-of-vibe-coded-apps); and Garry Tan reported that a quarter of YC's W25 batch shipped codebases that were ~95% AI-generated. None of these teams understood what they were running. That was the whole value proposition.

## The Wrong Ledger

So why doesn't this show up on any dashboard? Because we're booking it against the wrong ledger. Technical debt lives in the artifact — a linter can find it, a refactoring sprint can pay it, it sits still while you decide. Comprehension debt is different in kind, not degree. Margaret-Anne Storey calls the broader phenomenon [cognitive debt](https://getdx.com/blog/cognitive-debt-the-hidden-risk-in-ai-driven-software-development/): *technical debt lives in the code; cognitive debt lives in people.* It walks out the door at every resignation. It compounds silently while velocity charts climb. And no static analyzer will ever flag it, because the artifact it damages isn't in the repository.

> Technical debt shows up in the linter. Comprehension debt shows up in the exit interview.

It's worth saying that the concept has critics — [one thoughtful piece argues it's still a metaphor in search of testable diagnostics](https://www.fightforthehuman.com/we-need-more-than-a-metaphor-here-are-testable-diagnostics-for-comprehension-debt/), and proposes some. Good. A debt you can measure is a debt you can manage, and the measurements are exactly the ones teams already feel: how long until a new hire's first confident change? How many modules have zero people who can explain them? How often does a "trivial" change detonate something nobody predicted? If those numbers are drifting the wrong way while your merge rate sets records, you're reading this series at the right time.

## Booking the Debt

Here's the reframe the rest of the series builds on: comprehension is not overhead on the real work. Per [the pre-AI data](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/7997917/), it *was already* most of the real work — AI just made the imbalance explosive. So treat it the way you treat any other first-class engineering cost: give it a budget, an owner, and an enforcement mechanism. Book the debt.

That is a systems problem, not a discipline problem, and willpower-based solutions ("we'll review more carefully") lose to any opponent that ships at machine speed. The series takes the systemic route. Part 2 starts where most teams start — the markdown knowledge base — and shows why that beloved fix is *frozen context*: perfect for humans, palatable to AI, and structurally incapable of staying true, because markdown has no failing state. Part 3 argues boundaries beat willpower: plugin and module contracts as the only comprehension strategy that survives code you didn't write. Part 4 covers specs that can fail CI. Part 5 assembles those pieces into a metaframework — product management as a typed, validated catalog living in git. Part 6 is the Monday playbook for your team.

The teams that get this right won't be the ones that generate the most code — code generation is now table stakes, as evenly distributed as electricity. They'll be the ones whose *held theory* keeps pace with their repos: where every module has a human who can explain it, every invariant is written somewhere a machine can check it, and an engineer two years from now can change the payment path without archaeology. That future is buildable with tools that mostly already exist. The debt is real, but it is measurable — and what you can measure, you can pay down. Let's start the repayment plan.
